# Everdune Free-Play Foundation

Status: ACTIVE PRODUCT DIRECTION — 2026-09-28

## The pressure test

After a stressful day, a player should be able to launch Everdune, ignore the main story, spend 45 minutes doing whatever sounds good, and close the game feeling that they actually lived somewhere.

A successful session can be:
- fishing;
- woodcutting;
- mining;
- foraging;
- farming;
- cooking;
- building;
- decorating;
- mapping;
- talking to people;
- following an Echo;
- optional combat;
- wandering with no objective at all.

The game should not punish a player for choosing the quiet option.

## The Everdune loop

The systemic loop is:

**World → activity → skill → material/knowledge → relationship/home/world change → new possibility → return**

Every major activity should ideally produce three forms of progress:

1. **Immediate progress** — a material, meal, catch, XP or reward.
2. **Long-term progress** — skill mastery, collection, home development or equipment.
3. **World progress** — a relationship, memory, route, restoration, discovery or visible change.

## Skills

The initial Larkmere skill family is deliberately broad rather than MMO-like:

- Gathering — general interaction and resource handling.
- Woodcutting — trees, timber, forestry and later woodworking.
- Mining — stone, ore, cave resources and archaeology.
- Foraging — berries, herbs, mushrooms and environmental clues.
- Fishing — river knowledge, catches, trophies and cooking.
- Farming — planting, waiting, harvesting and seasonal home food.
- Cooking — turns what the player finds into useful meals and gifts.
- Crafting — meaningful objects rather than recipe spam.
- Building — improvements to the home and eventually the valley.
- Wayfinding — map knowledge, shortcuts, landmarks and lost roads.
- Memory — Echo discovery and knowledge.
- Combat — optional mastery for players who want intensity.

Skills are not separate minigames. They are lenses through which the same world becomes more interesting.

## Activity design

Activities are small repeatable reasons to spend time in a place.

Each activity should answer:

- Why would I do this for five minutes?
- Why would I improve at it for fifty hours?
- What can it connect to?
- What can I discover while doing it?
- What evidence can it leave behind?

Examples:

### Fishing
Fish → cooking → food → gifts → NPC relationships → trophies → home display → river knowledge → rare Echoes.

### Woodcutting
Trees → timber → building → furniture → home identity → valley restoration → new routes.

### Mining
Stone/ore → crafting/building → deeper Bellroot access → archaeology → Echoes → rare materials.

### Foraging
Plants → cooking/farming → seasonal collections → environmental clues → hidden locations.

### Farming
Seeds → garden → food/decor → home identity → gifts → future orchard systems.

### Wayfinding
Old roads → map fragments → shortcuts → safer travel → lost places → historical Echoes.

## Chill rules

Everdune deliberately avoids survival pressure that turns relaxation into obligation.

Do not require:
- constant hunger management;
- thirst meters;
- durability anxiety;
- mandatory sleep;
- daily chore streaks;
- punishing resource starvation;
- excessive inventory micromanagement.

Energy should create texture, not stop the evening. When energy is low, walking, talking, fishing, decorating, reading and exploring should remain valid.

## The ten-reasons rule

A player who chooses one activity should naturally encounter other possibilities.

Example: "Tonight I will fish."

That choice can lead to:
1. a different fish appearing because of weather;
2. a cooking ingredient;
3. a rare catch;
4. a trophy;
5. a river Echo;
6. an NPC comment;
7. an unusual ripple;
8. a map clue;
9. a new shortcut;
10. a reason to return tomorrow.

The player is never required to follow those threads.

## World consequence

A player action should leave evidence whenever practical.

Examples:
- restore a route → NPCs use it;
- harvest and replant → the garden visibly changes;
- build a workshop → the home gains a functional station;
- display a fish → the home tells a story;
- map an old road → the player has a persistent shortcut/knowledge marker;
- discover an Echo → NPC dialogue can remember it.

## Home

The home is the player's autobiography.

A fisher should have a different-looking home from an explorer, farmer, builder or lore-focused player.

Home progression is therefore:
- functional;
- visual;
- collectible;
- social;
- historical.

A home improvement should make the next session easier or richer, not simply raise a number.

## NPC lives

NPCs need:
- routines;
- favourite places;
- relationships;
- memories;
- personal goals;
- contextual dialogue;
- responses to world changes.

A player should eventually be able to ask, "What is Mara doing today?" and have the answer come from simulation rather than a quest flag.

## Echoes

Echoes are not collectible lore cards.

They are systemic memories.

A strong Echo should:
- reveal history;
- grant knowledge;
- change a place;
- alter a relationship;
- reveal a route;
- unlock an activity;
- change dialogue;
- create a future Echo;
- or otherwise alter the player's understanding of the valley.

## Content density rule

Every Larkmere location should have:

**place + resource + skill + person + story + Echo + secret + restoration + reason to return.**

A small dense valley is more valuable than a huge empty map.

## Session targets

- 10 minutes: one satisfying activity.
- 30 minutes: meaningful progress.
- 45 minutes: self-directed evening.
- 60 minutes: multiple connected activities.
- 2 hours: deep project or exploration.
- 5 hours: major home/world project.
- 100 hours: mastery and collection.
- 1,000 hours: self-directed life in the world.

The long-term goal is not to force retention.

It is to make returning feel natural because there is always something the player genuinely wants to do.
